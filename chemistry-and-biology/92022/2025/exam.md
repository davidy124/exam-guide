## QUESTION ONE: Chromosomes, genes, alleles, and DNA

The cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene in humans is responsible for mucus production in the human body.

The normal allele (F) is dominant over the mutant allele (f) in humans. Individuals who are homozygous recessive will develop cystic fibrosis, a condition where too much mucus is produced, which can cause difficulty breathing.

**Model Answer:**

Chromosomes are long strands of DNA tightly wound together.

DNA is a large molecule coiled into a double-helix structure, made up of sugar, phosphate, and a base. There are four bases that always pair (A–T and G–C).

A gene is a specific section of the DNA sequence that codes for a specific trait. An allele is a different version of the same gene, providing variation in the characteristic/phenotype.

**Example (CFTR context):** Human genomes have two copies of chromosome 7. A specific section of chromosome 7 contains the CFTR gene. The normal version is the "normal allele (F)". A slight change in the base sequence gives a different version of the gene — the "mutant allele (f)".

<summary>📋 Answer &amp; Marking Criteria</summary>

**Model Answer:**

**Genotype** is the allele combination (for a specific trait) at a particular locus on a chromosome. Individuals can have one of three CFTR genotypes: FF, Ff, or ff.

**Phenotype** is the physical expression of the genotype — the observable characteristics or traits resulting from the interaction of genotype with the environment. The FF and Ff genotypes express a phenotype of normal mucus production. The ff genotype expresses a phenotype of excessive mucus production (cystic fibrosis).

**Genetic variation** is the difference between the genes/alleles in a population. For the CFTR gene, variation is observed in the two alleles: normal allele (F) and mutant allele (f).

**Heritable variation** is variation in traits/alleles/genes that can be passed on to offspring — the mutation must have occurred in a sex cell (gamete).

**Non-heritable variation** comes from environmental effects on somatic cells and cannot be passed on — the mutation occurred in a somatic cell.

The initial mutation of the CFTR gene **must have occurred in the gametic cell**, because it has been passed on for many generations within the population.

**Model Answer:**

**Evidence from parents (1 and 2):**
Individuals 1 and 2 must be heterozygous (Ff) because:
- Individual 7 is unaffected, so must have inherited at least one dominant F allele from a parent.
- Individuals 5 and 6 are affected (ff), so both parents must each have passed on an f allele → both parents must be Ff.

**Evidence from children (11 and 12):**
Both 7 and 8 must be heterozygous (Ff) because:
- They are both unaffected.
- They have two affected children (11 and 12 are ff), meaning both parents must each contribute an f allele → both 7 and 8 must carry an f allele → both are Ff.

This is consistent with the Punnett square (Ff × Ff → 1 FF : 2 Ff : 1 ff), which shows a 1 in 4 chance of producing an ff (affected) child.

## QUESTION THREE: Continuation in the population

The normal CFTR allele (F) is dominant over the mutant allele (f). Homozygous recessive individuals will develop cystic fibrosis. Homozygous dominant and heterozygous individuals will not be affected.

<summary>📋 Answer &amp; Marking Criteria</summary>

**Model Answer:**

**Meiosis** is a process of cell division producing four genetically non-identical daughter cells.
- **Crossing over**: homologous chromosomes exchange genetic material, producing new unique combinations of alleles.
- **Independent assortment**: homologous chromosomes line up randomly before separating into gametes.
- These processes mean there is a chance the f allele is passed to gametes and remains in the gene pool.

**Sexual reproduction** is a biological process where offspring are produced through the combination of genetic material from two parent organisms. **Fertilisation** is the fusion of two gametes (sperm and egg).
- Even if both parents do not have cystic fibrosis, they may be carriers (Ff) and have a chance of passing the f allele on to offspring.

**Non-random mating** (selective mating) refers to mating that has not occurred by chance — each mate has 'chosen' the other for a particular reason (e.g. physical or genetic traits).
- This could happen in small populations where there is a higher chance of two heterozygotes mating (inbreeding / mating with close relatives / second cousins), increasing the chance of the f allele being passed on.

<details>
<summary>📋 Answer &amp; Marking Criteria</summary>

**Model Answer:**

**Population** = a group of individuals of the same species living in the same area (capable of interbreeding).

**Why f allele may increase:**
- The f allele is disadvantageous in homozygous recessive individuals (ff → cystic fibrosis).
- However, it is neither advantageous nor disadvantageous in heterozygotes (Ff).
- Heterozygotes may have increased resistance to certain infectious diseases (e.g. cholera or typhoid). If this advantage improves reproductive success, it increases the survival chances of heterozygotes, allowing them to reproduce and pass on the f allele → f allele increases in the population.
- In smaller populations, more heterozygotes may mate together more often → higher frequency of the f allele (random sampling effects / genetic drift in a small gene pool).

**Why an increase in f allele frequency does NOT always increase cystic fibrosis phenotype frequency:**
- The dominant allele (F) completely masks the recessive allele (f) in heterozygotes.
- It is possible for the number of heterozygotes (Ff) to increase — therefore increasing the number of recessive alleles — **without** increasing the number of cystic fibrosis phenotypes.
- For example: if there are fewer FF genotypes but more Ff genotypes, the f allele frequency rises, but CF phenotype frequency stays the same (only ff individuals are affected).

**Why f allele may decrease:**
- With increased availability of gene tracking and genetic testing, the recessive f allele may decrease in frequency over coming generations.
- Prospective parents with a family history of cystic fibrosis may have embryos tested (e.g. IVF pre-implantation genetic diagnosis). If the f allele is present, they may choose not to implant the embryo or may terminate a pregnancy. This could reduce the f allele in the population.
