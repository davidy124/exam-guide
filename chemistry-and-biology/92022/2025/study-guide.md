## Core Vocabulary — Must Know Precisely

> **The #1 reason candidates lose marks: using "gene" and "allele" interchangeably.** These are different things. So are "genotype" and "phenotype".

| Term | Precise Definition | Common Mistake |
|---|---|---|
| **DNA** | Large double-helix molecule made of sugar, phosphate, and bases (A–T, G–C pairs) | Saying DNA "is" a chromosome |
| **Chromosome** | Long strand of tightly wound DNA; humans have 46 (23 pairs) | Confusing chromosome with gene |
| **Gene** | A specific section of a DNA sequence that codes for a specific trait | Using "gene" when you mean "allele" |
| **Allele** | A different version of the same gene | Using "allele" when you mean "gene" |
| **Genotype** | The allele combination for a specific trait at a locus (e.g. FF, Ff, ff) | Confusing with phenotype |
| **Phenotype** | The physical/observable expression of the genotype (influenced by environment) | Confusing with genotype |
| **Mutation** | A permanent base change in the sequence of a DNA strand | Saying mutation = allele |
| **Heritable variation** | Variation that can be passed to offspring (mutation in gamete/sex cell) | Confusing with non-heritable |
| **Non-heritable variation** | Variation from environmental effects on somatic cells; cannot be passed on | Thinking all mutations are heritable |
| **Dominant** | An allele whose trait is expressed even with only one copy (F) | |
| **Recessive** | An allele whose trait is only expressed when homozygous (f) | |
| **Homozygous** | Two identical alleles at a locus (FF or ff) | |
| **Heterozygous** | Two different alleles at a locus (Ff) — also called a carrier | |
| **Carrier** | A heterozygous individual (Ff) who does not have the disease but can pass on f | |
| **Locus** | The specific location of a gene on a chromosome | |
| **Gamete** | A sex cell (sperm or egg) — haploid, carries one allele per locus | |
| **Population** | A group of individuals of the same species in the same area (capable of interbreeding) | |
| **Gene pool** | All the alleles present in a population | |
| **Allele frequency** | How common a particular allele is in a population | |

## CFTR Gene — Context Facts

- CFTR = cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator
- Located on **chromosome 7**
- Controls mucus production in the human body
- **Normal allele: F** (dominant)
- **Mutant allele: f** (recessive)

| Genotype | Phenotype |
|---|---|
| FF | Normal mucus production (unaffected) |
| Ff | Normal mucus production (unaffected — carrier) |
| ff | Excessive mucus production = **cystic fibrosis** |

> The original mutation (F → f) **must have occurred in a gametic cell** — because it has been passed on across many generations. If it had occurred in a somatic cell, it would be non-heritable.

## Question One — Knowledge Points

### 1A. The DNA → Chromosome → Gene → Allele Hierarchy

```
DNA
 └─ wound tightly → Chromosome
                      └─ specific section → Gene (codes for one trait)
                                              └─ different version → Allele
```

**Key relationship to explain:**
- Chromosomes are made of DNA
- Genes are sections of DNA on chromosomes
- Alleles are variants of the same gene at the same locus
- Alleles cause variation in phenotype

**For Merit:** You must *explain the relationship*, not just define each term separately. Use the CFTR example to connect them: chromosome 7 → CFTR gene → F allele vs f allele.

**For Excellence:** Link all four in a continuous explanation with clear biological examples. Examples do **not** have to be CFTR — blood groups, eye colour etc. are valid.

---

### 1B. Mutation, Genotype/Phenotype, Heritable/Non-heritable

**Three parts to cover in one answer:**

**Part 1 — Mutation:**
- Permanent base change in DNA sequence
- For CFTR: the F allele base sequence changed → became the f allele

**Part 2 — Phenotype vs Genotype:**
- Genotype: the allele combination (FF / Ff / ff)
- Phenotype: the observable result (normal mucus / cystic fibrosis)
- FF and Ff have the same phenotype despite different genotypes → dominance
- Only ff shows the cystic fibrosis phenotype

**Part 3 — Heritable vs Non-heritable:**
- Heritable: mutation in gamete → can be inherited by offspring
- Non-heritable: mutation in somatic cell → stays with that individual only
- The CFTR mutation is heritable → must have occurred in a gamete

**Also needed for Merit/Excellence:**
- Somatic vs gametic mutation distinction
- The initial F→f mutation must have been gametic because it persists across generations

## Question Two — Knowledge Points

### 2A. Reading Pedigree Charts

**Rules for interpreting pedigrees:**
1. Squares = male, Circles = female
2. Shaded = affected (has the condition)
3. Lines between individuals = mating pair; vertical lines = offspring
4. Genotypes contain **TWO alleles** (a common mistake is writing only one)

**Working out genotypes from a pedigree:**
- Affected individual (cystic fibrosis) → **must be ff**
- If a person is unaffected but has an affected child → **must be Ff** (carrier)
- If two unaffected parents have an affected child → **both parents must be Ff**
- Unaffected person with no affected offspring → could be FF **or** Ff (cannot confirm)

**For this pedigree:**
| Individual | Genotype | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ff | Unaffected, but has affected children (5 and 6) → must carry f |
| 2 | Ff | Same reasoning as 1 |
| 3 | FF or Ff | Unaffected; no affected offspring → cannot confirm |
| 4 | FF or Ff | Unaffected, child of 1 & 2 → could have inherited f; no affected offspring to confirm |
| 5 | ff | Affected → homozygous recessive |
| 6 | ff | Affected → homozygous recessive |
| 7 | Ff | Unaffected, but has affected children (11 and 12) → must carry f |
| 8 | Ff | Unaffected, but has affected children (11 and 12) → must carry f |
| 9 | FF or Ff | Unaffected; no affected offspring |
| 10 | FF or Ff | Unaffected; no affected offspring |
| 11 | ff | Affected |
| 12 | ff | Affected |

---

### 2B. Punnett Squares

**Standard Ff × Ff cross (the key cross for this exam):**

|   | F  | f  |
|---|----|----|
| **F** | FF | Ff |
| **f** | Ff | ff |

**Result:** 1 FF : 2 Ff : 1 ff → **25% chance of affected offspring**

**How to use Punnett squares as evidence:**
- To confirm Individual 7 is Ff: show the Ff × Ff cross for parents 1 & 2 → proves 7 could inherit Ff
- Then show Ff × Ff cross for 7 & 8 → proves they can produce ff children (11 & 12)
- The existence of affected offspring (ff) *proves* both parents must each carry at least one f allele → both must be Ff

**For Excellence:** Use evidence from BOTH directions — parents of 7 (1 & 2) AND children of 7 & 8 (11 & 12). Stating only one direction limits you to Merit.

---

### 2C. Purpose of Genetic Tracking

**Why track Individual 4's genotype?**

Individual 4's genotype (FF or Ff) **cannot be confirmed** from existing pedigree information because:
- Her children (9 and 10) are both unaffected → they could be FF or Ff
- No affected child yet to prove 4 carries f
- 4 could only be confirmed as Ff if 3 & 4 produce an affected child

**Purposes of tracking (think at multiple levels):**

| Level | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Individual 4 | Know if she is a carrier → informs decision about having more children |
| Individual 3 & 4 as a couple | If both Ff, they have a 25% chance of an affected child → family planning |
| Children (9 & 10) | If 4 is Ff, 9 & 10 may also be carriers → informs their future reproductive decisions |
| Medical/societal | Develop targeted treatments; advance understanding of inheritance; genetic counselling |

**For Excellence:** Must reference *parents*, *their offspring*, AND *future offspring/broader implications* — not just one level.

## Question Three — Knowledge Points

### 3A. How Meiosis, Sexual Reproduction, and Non-random Mating Continue the f Allele

**Meiosis:**
- Cell division producing 4 genetically non-identical daughter cells (gametes)
- **Crossing over** (prophase I): homologous chromosomes exchange sections → new allele combinations
- **Independent assortment** (metaphase I): chromosomes align randomly → further variation in gametes
- Result: gametes have unique allele combinations → f allele has a chance of being passed to gametes

**Sexual reproduction:**
- Two parent organisms combine genetic material
- **Fertilisation**: fusion of sperm and egg (two haploid cells → one diploid zygote)
- Even if neither parent *has* cystic fibrosis, carrier parents (Ff) can pass the f allele to offspring
- Two Ff carriers have a 25% chance of producing an ff (affected) child each time

**Non-random mating:**
- Mating that is not by chance — individuals select mates for specific reasons (physical, genetic, cultural)
- In small populations: higher chance that two related individuals (both Ff) mate → inbreeding / selective mating with relatives
- This increases the probability of the f allele being passed on and even of ff offspring appearing

**Linking all three for Excellence:**
Meiosis generates gametes with a chance of carrying f → sexual reproduction combines two gametes (both possibly carrying f from Ff parents) → non-random mating in small populations increases the likelihood that two f-carrying gametes meet → f allele persists and may increase in the gene pool.

---

### 3B. Why the f Allele Remains (or Changes) in the Population

**Why it may INCREASE:**

1. **Heterozygote advantage:** Carriers (Ff) may have increased resistance to infectious diseases (e.g. cholera, typhoid). If Ff individuals have better reproductive success → more Ff individuals → more f alleles in the population.

2. **Genetic drift (small populations):** In small gene pools, random chance means two heterozygotes may mate more often, raising f allele frequency purely by chance.

**Why it may DECREASE:**

3. **Genetic testing / IVF screening:** Parents with CF family history may screen embryos. If the f allele is detected, they may not implant the embryo (IVF) or may terminate a pregnancy → f allele is removed from the gene pool over generations. More likely in populations with access to this technology.

**Key concept for Excellence — Why more f alleles ≠ more CF phenotype:**

> The dominant F allele completely **masks** the f allele in heterozygotes (Ff).

- If the population shifts from many FF individuals to many Ff individuals:
  - The **number of f alleles increases** (Ff carries one f)
  - But the **number of ff individuals stays the same or even decreases**
  - Because Ff individuals are phenotypically normal — they don't show cystic fibrosis
- So you can have rising f allele frequency **without** rising CF disease frequency

## Common Mistakes — What Not Achieved Candidates Did

> These are the exact errors identified in the 2025 assessment report. Avoid all of these.

1. **Confusing "gene" and "allele"** — most damaging error; limits you to Achievement at best
2. **Confusing "genotype" and "phenotype"** — e.g. writing "the genotype of the individual is cystic fibrosis"
3. **Confusing "non-random mating" with "inbreeding"** — non-random mating is the broader term; inbreeding is one example of it
4. **Writing genotypes with only one allele** — e.g. writing "F" instead of "FF" or "Ff"
5. **Restating the question** — writing back what the question says without adding any biological explanation
6. **Copying the stimulus** — copying information from the exam paper without applying biological knowledge
7. **Leaving questions blank** — always attempt; partial marks are available at every level
8. **Not linking DNA → chromosome → gene → allele** — these must be connected, not listed separately

| Grade | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| **Not Achieved** | Defines terms incorrectly or not at all; confuses key vocabulary; copies stimulus |

## Additional Review Areas

### A. How to Write for Merit and Excellence

**Trigger words in questions:**
| Word | What it requires |
|---|---|
| *Define* | One-sentence precise definition |
| *Describe* | What it is + key features |
| *Explain* | What + WHY it happens |
| *Discuss* | Multiple angles, consider different possibilities, weigh up |
| *Evaluate* | Make a judgement supported by evidence |

**Bullet point answers vs. flowing paragraphs:**
- Achievement: bullet points are fine
- Merit/Excellence: flowing sentences that *link* ideas score higher than a list of correct but isolated facts

**Key phrase to use:** "This means that..." or "Therefore..." — these force you to make causal links, not just list facts.

---

### B. Pedigree Reading — Step-by-Step Strategy

1. Find any **affected individuals** (shaded) → their genotype is **ff**
2. Work backwards: affected individual's parents must each carry at least one f → both parents are **Ff**
3. Work forwards: any unaffected sibling of an ff individual *could be* FF or Ff
4. Check children: if an unaffected person has an affected child, they must be **Ff**
5. If no affected children → genotype cannot be confirmed (could be FF or Ff)

> Never write "Individual X is FF" unless you can prove they cannot carry an f allele. If there is any doubt, write "FF or Ff".

---

### C. Meiosis vs Mitosis — Know the Difference

| | Meiosis | Mitosis |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Produce gametes (sex cells) | Growth, repair, replacement |
| Divisions | 2 | 1 |
| Daughter cells | 4, haploid (n) | 2, diploid (2n) |
| Genetic identity | Non-identical (crossing over + independent assortment) | Identical |
| Where | Gonads (ovaries, testes) | Throughout body |

> Only meiosis is relevant to 92022 — it's the process that creates the gametes that carry alleles to offspring.

---

### D. Allele Frequency Concepts — Population Thinking

For Q3, think at the population level, not just the individual level:

- **Allele frequency** = how common an allele is across all individuals in a population
- The f allele exists in the population **mostly hidden inside Ff heterozygotes** — these individuals are phenotypically normal and reproduce freely
- Natural selection acts on **phenotype**, not genotype — since Ff looks normal, the f allele is "hidden" from selection pressure
- This is the core reason the f allele persists despite causing a serious disease when homozygous

**Heterozygote advantage** (also called balanced polymorphism):
- If Ff individuals have higher survival/reproductive success than FF or ff in certain environments (e.g. resistance to cholera)
- Then both F and f alleles are maintained in the population — neither completely disappears

---

### E. Genetic Testing — Broader Implications

These points are relevant to Q2c and Q3b for Excellence responses:

- **Pre-implantation genetic diagnosis (PGD):** Testing embryos during IVF before implantation
- **Carrier testing:** Testing individuals (e.g. Individual 4) to determine if they carry the f allele
- **Genetic counselling:** Helping families understand inheritance risk and make reproductive decisions
- **Ethical considerations:** Knowing your carrier status may affect decisions about having children
- **Population-level impact:** Widespread genetic testing could reduce f allele frequency over generations in populations with access to the technology
